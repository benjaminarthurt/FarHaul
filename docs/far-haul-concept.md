# FAR HAUL

«Build your ship. Haul the freight. Push the frontier.»

## Overview

Far Haul is a first-person open-world science-fiction freight, ship-building, and exploration game set during humanity's expansion across nearby star systems.

The player begins as an independent freight operator with a small, inexpensive ship and enough money to keep it flying. Early life is practical: accept contracts, load cargo, calculate whether a job is worth the fuel, maintain the ship, and move goods between established worlds.

There is no requirement to become a soldier, chosen hero, or savior of civilization.

You are a ship owner.

What happens after that is largely up to you.

Run freight between major industrial worlds. Supply remote colonies. Carry passengers. Salvage abandoned ships. Transport oversized equipment. Build specialized vessels. Survey uninhabited planets. Establish routes through barely explored systems. Or leave the charts entirely and discover places no human has visited before.

The farther you travel, the less infrastructure exists to support you.

Eventually there is no repair yard.

No fuel depot.

No navigation beacon.

No rescue service.

Just your ship and whatever you remembered to bring.

---

## The Ship Is the Game

The player's ship is not a menu, inventory screen, or interchangeable vehicle.

It is a physical place.

Every compartment built by the player exists inside the game world. The player can leave the pilot's seat and walk through the ship while underway, inspect machinery, enter cargo holds, access engineering spaces, use airlocks, visit crew quarters, and physically reach damaged equipment.

If the ship becomes forty meters longer, it takes longer to walk from the cockpit to engineering.

If the player installs another cargo bay, that space physically exists.

If a meteor penetrates the hull, the resulting hole exists where the impact occurred.

If something outside breaks, someone has to go outside and fix it.

The ship gradually becomes the player's home, workplace, transportation, investment, and record of their journey.

---

## Function Over Form

Ships in Far Haul are unapologetically industrial.

They are boxes, frames, tanks, engines, radiators, cargo racks, antennas, landing gear, pipes, pressure vessels, and machinery.

They are designed to work.

A typical ship might consist of a cockpit attached to a habitation module, several rectangular cargo compartments, an engineering section, fuel tanks, and engines bolted to a structural frame.

There is no requirement that ships be sleek or symmetrical.

A ship only needs to function.

Players can build compact atmospheric freighters, long interstellar cargo vessels, tankers, survey ships, passenger transports, salvage vessels, mobile workshops, or enormous orbital freighters carrying smaller landing craft.

A strange-looking ship that obeys the engineering rules is a perfectly valid ship.

---

## Modular Ship Construction

Ships are assembled from structural and functional components rather than selected from a catalog of completed spacecraft.

Construction can include:

- structural frames
- pressure hull sections
- floors and bulkheads
- cockpits and bridges
- cargo holds
- external cargo racks
- crew compartments
- airlocks
- landing gear
- reactors
- batteries
- fuel tanks
- engines
- maneuvering thrusters
- life-support systems
- cooling systems and radiators
- navigation equipment
- sensors
- communications equipment
- workshops
- medical facilities
- docking systems
- specialized industrial equipment

Components occupy physical space and have mass.

Their location matters.

A poorly balanced ship can still fly, but its control systems may consume additional maneuvering propellant compensating for an offset center of mass.

More cargo requires more thrust.

More thrust requires more power and fuel.

More power produces more heat.

More crew requires more life support.

Every improvement introduces engineering decisions.

The goal is not to force players to become aerospace engineers. Ship systems should be understandable visually and logically while providing significant depth for players who want to optimize them.

---

## Freight

Freight is the economic foundation of Far Haul.

Civilization requires things to move.

Food has to reach stations that cannot grow enough of it. Mining colonies need machinery. New settlements need construction materials. Hospitals need medical supplies. Reactors need replacement components. Factories need raw materials.

The player makes money by moving those things.

Cargo has more than monetary value and mass. It has physical characteristics.

A shipment may require refrigeration.

Another may require radiation shielding.

Some cargo must remain pressurized.

Some may be hazardous.

Some may be fragile.

Some may simply be enormous.

A twenty-meter industrial machine cannot magically disappear into an inventory screen. The player needs a ship capable of carrying it.

---

## Standardized Cargo

Interstellar commerce relies heavily on standardized cargo modules.

Containers can be moved between ships, stations, surface vehicles, warehouses, and orbital facilities.

Small ships may carry several containers internally.

Larger freighters may carry dozens or hundreds on external racks.

Bulk carriers may transport enormous quantities of standardized freight without maintaining atmospheric pressure around it.

Specialized containers can include:

- refrigerated cargo
- liquids
- gases
- hazardous materials
- high-security cargo
- livestock
- fragile scientific equipment
- radioactive materials
- passenger modules

Cargo capacity therefore becomes a physical design problem rather than simply an inventory statistic.

---

## Loading and Unloading

Cargo exists in the world.

At developed ports, automated systems and heavy equipment handle most routine loading.

At smaller frontier settlements, the player may watch forklifts, cranes, loaders, and workers physically move cargo.

Remote destinations may have almost no infrastructure.

Delivering a large piece of equipment to an undeveloped planet could require bringing the equipment necessary to unload it.

The destination matters just as much as the cargo.

---

## The Human Sphere

Human civilization occupies a relatively small region of space.

Dozens of systems have permanent populations, but development varies enormously.

There are densely populated core systems with enormous orbital infrastructure and constant traffic.

There are established colonies with cities, industries, shipyards, and regular freight service.

There are frontier settlements where only a few thousand people live.

Beyond them are systems that have been surveyed but never settled.

Beyond those are stars appearing in astronomical catalogs but never visited by humans.

And eventually the player reaches the edge of humanity's actual charts.

---

## Core Systems

Core systems represent mature interstellar civilization.

They contain busy ports, orbital stations, established shipping lanes, customs authorities, repair facilities, fuel suppliers, passenger terminals, ship dealerships, warehouses, and enormous industrial economies.

Operating here is relatively safe.

If something breaks, somebody can fix it.

If the player runs out of fuel, assistance is available.

If cargo is damaged, insurance may cover it.

Competition is also substantial.

Freight rates are predictable and margins can be thin.

Core systems are where players learn how to operate a business.

They are not necessarily where fortunes are made.

---

## The Frontier

As the player travels outward, infrastructure becomes progressively thinner.

A frontier system might contain one inhabited planet.

Another may have only a mining station orbiting an otherwise uninhabited world.

A settlement may have fuel but no major repair facility.

Another may have a landing pad but no orbital station.

Supply ships might arrive once every several months.

Freight prices become higher because transportation becomes harder.

So do the risks.

The frontier is where freight and exploration begin to overlap.

---

## Beyond the Routes

Eventually established freight routes end.

The player can continue.

Surveyed systems may contain known planets and basic astronomical data but no permanent human presence.

There are no cargo contracts because nobody lives there.

The reason to continue becomes exploration.

A survey ship requires different priorities than a commercial freighter.

Cargo capacity may be sacrificed for:

- additional fuel
- water
- food
- oxygen reserves
- spare parts
- redundant systems
- scientific equipment
- surface vehicles
- emergency supplies

Every kilogram devoted to survival is capacity that cannot generate freight revenue.

Exploration has a cost.

---

## Unknown Systems

Eventually even survey information disappears.

The navigation computer may know that a star exists because astronomers observed it from elsewhere, but nobody knows what surrounds it.

The player becomes the survey mission.

Upon arrival, sensors begin constructing the system map.

Planets are detected.

Moons are identified.

Orbits are calculated.

Atmospheres are analyzed.

Potential landing sites appear.

Resources are discovered.

Some systems will be extraordinary.

Others will contain almost nothing worth returning for.

Both outcomes are important.

Exploration means uncertainty.

---

## Planetary Exploration

Many solid planets and moons can be visited directly.

The player can enter orbit, identify a landing location, descend through the atmosphere if necessary, and physically land the ship.

The ship remains exactly where it was left.

From there, exploration continues at smaller scales.

The player can travel using:

Ship → Surface Vehicle → EVA Suit

A rover can cover large distances.

Difficult terrain may require continuing on foot.

Remote exploration therefore becomes a logistical exercise.

How far can the player travel from the ship?

How much oxygen remains?

How much battery capacity does the rover have?

Is a storm approaching?

Can the ship safely take off again?

The environment itself becomes part of the challenge.

---

## Discoveries Have Value

Survey information is economically valuable.

A player returning from an unexplored system may possess information about:

- mineral deposits
- water
- atmospheric composition
- potentially habitable environments
- useful orbital locations
- geological anomalies
- biological discoveries
- viable settlement locations
- safe navigation routes

Information can be sold, licensed, published, or retained.

A mining company may pay heavily for exclusive mineral survey data.

A scientific organization may finance another expedition.

A government or settlement organization may become interested in colonization.

Exploration therefore feeds directly back into the economy.

---

## Exploration Creates Freight

One of the central ideas behind Far Haul is that the world changes because of exploration.

The player may discover a promising planet with:

Population: 0

Later, a scientific expedition arrives.

Population: 14

They need supplies.

The player delivers equipment.

Additional researchers arrive.

A permanent research station is established.

A mining operation begins elsewhere on the planet.

An orbital fuel depot appears.

Settlers arrive.

Years later, the system may have thousands of residents and regular commercial traffic.

Eventually other freight companies establish routes there.

The place no longer needs the player.

That is success.

The player has watched an unknown point on the map become part of civilization.

---

## A Living Economy

Freight demand should emerge from what locations actually produce and consume.

Agricultural worlds export food.

Industrial worlds consume raw materials and export manufactured equipment.

Mining settlements export ore while importing nearly everything required to survive.

Growing colonies consume enormous amounts of construction material.

Disasters can temporarily disrupt supply chains.

A damaged reactor may create urgent demand for replacement equipment.

A poor harvest may increase food imports.

A newly discovered mineral deposit can cause a temporary economic boom.

The player can simply accept available contracts or learn these patterns and operate as an independent trader.

---

## Ship Specialization

There should be no universally best spacecraft.

A vessel optimized for one role should make compromises elsewhere.

### Atmospheric Freighter

Compact, reinforced and capable of carrying freight directly to planetary surfaces.

Excellent for frontier deliveries.

Limited capacity compared with orbital freighters.

### Bulk Freighter

Built almost entirely around cargo.

Efficient between major ports.

Large vessels may be incapable of planetary landing.

### Tanker

Carries liquids, gases, fuels, or other bulk commodities.

Requires specialized handling equipment.

### Survey Vessel

Long range, extensive sensors, redundant systems, workshops and surface exploration equipment.

Expensive to operate without exploration contracts.

### Salvage Vessel

Equipped with powerful maneuvering systems, storage, cutting equipment, workshops and external attachment points.

### Passenger/Cargo Vessel

Combines freight operations with passenger transportation.

Requires additional life support and accommodations.

### Heavy Transport

Designed around cargo that doesn't fit inside normal containers.

The ship may effectively be built around whatever enormous object it is transporting.

---

## Orbital Freighters

Eventually ships can become too large and heavy for routine planetary operations.

That creates another stage of progression.

Instead of forcing every spacecraft to land, large vessels remain in orbit.

Cargo moves between the surface and ship using smaller shuttles.

A late-game player's vessel may effectively become a mobile commercial base containing:

- bridge
- engineering
- large crew quarters
- workshops
- enormous cargo capacity
- external container racks
- docking facilities
- survey equipment
- one or more atmospheric shuttles

The player has progressed from driving a space van to commanding something resembling an interstellar container ship.

---

## Physical Damage

Ships don't merely have a generic health bar.

Damage affects components and locations.

A micrometeorite might penetrate a cargo compartment.

The ship detects pressure loss.

Bulkheads close.

The affected compartment depressurizes.

The player can inspect the ship's systems, determine where the failure occurred, put on a suit, isolate the damaged section, and repair it.

An engine failure means an engine has failed.

A damaged radiator affects cooling.

A destroyed antenna affects communications.

A damaged landing leg can make landing dangerous.

Failures create problems that can be understood by looking at the ship.

---

## EVA

Spacewalking is a normal part of operating spacecraft.

External equipment requires inspection and maintenance.

The player can leave through an airlock and move around the exterior of the ship using handholds, magnetic boots, tethers, or maneuvering equipment.

EVA may be necessary to:

- repair hull damage
- replace sensors
- inspect engines
- repair radiators
- connect external cargo
- investigate unknown objects
- board abandoned spacecraft
- perform emergency repairs

The ship the player constructed becomes the environment they physically climb across.

Looking back through a cockpit window and seeing the interior of the ship from outside reinforces that it is one continuous physical object.

---

## Maintenance

Ships require maintenance without turning the game into constant busywork.

Components wear.

Filters need replacement.

Engines require servicing.

Landing gear takes abuse.

Cheap components fail more frequently.

Temporary repairs can keep a ship operating until proper facilities become available.

The farther the player travels from civilization, the more important maintenance becomes.

A minor problem near Earth is an inconvenience.

The same problem six unexplored systems beyond the nearest settlement can threaten an entire expedition.

---

## Manufacturers

The universe should feel manufactured rather than artist-designed.

Different companies produce components with recognizable engineering philosophies.

One manufacturer may produce inexpensive, standardized equipment that is easy to repair.

Another builds massive industrial hardware famous for durability.

Another specializes in lightweight exploration components.

Military-surplus systems may be exceptionally rugged but inefficient.

Premium manufacturers may produce compact, quiet, highly integrated equipment that is expensive and difficult to repair without specialized parts.

Experienced players should eventually recognize equipment visually.

A reactor isn't simply "Reactor Level 4."

It's a particular machine built by a particular company with known strengths and weaknesses.

---

## Interior Design

Ship interiors remain practical.

Space is expensive.

Small ships are cramped.

Pipes, conduits, access panels, storage compartments and machinery occupy valuable space.

Early in the game, the player's sleeping area may simply be a bunk mounted behind the cockpit.

The galley might consist of a heater and small refrigerator.

The bathroom is barely large enough to stand inside.

Progression makes seemingly mundane improvements meaningful.

Eventually the player can afford a private cabin.

A real bed.

A desk.

A shower.

A galley where two people can stand simultaneously.

Comfort becomes another resource the player can choose to invest in.

---

## Crew

Larger ships can support additional crew.

Crew members are people who physically live aboard the vessel.

They require sleeping accommodations, food, water, life support, and space.

An engineer can monitor machinery and perform maintenance.

A navigator can improve route planning.

A loadmaster can improve cargo operations.

A scientist can operate survey equipment.

Crew should reduce workload and expand capabilities rather than merely provide percentage bonuses.

The player can still operate alone if they deliberately design a ship around doing so.

---

## Passengers

People also need transportation.

Passenger contracts range from ordinary travel between established systems to transporting specialists into frontier territory.

A passenger requires somewhere to live during the trip.

A cheap operator might provide a tiny bunk.

A larger vessel might contain private cabins.

Passenger expectations vary based on ticket price and journey length.

Eventually passenger transportation could become an entire business model of its own.

---

## Salvage

Space contains abandoned equipment, damaged vessels, discarded stages, failed probes and wreckage.

Some salvage opportunities are known.

Others are discovered accidentally.

The player can board derelict spacecraft, determine what happened, restore systems, recover cargo, remove valuable components, or potentially recover the vessel itself.

Salvage integrates naturally with EVA, engineering and ship construction.

A valuable component discovered aboard a wreck can potentially become part of the player's own ship.

---

## Story

Far Haul does not require the player to save the galaxy.

Its larger story is humanity's continuing expansion.

Individual locations and characters provide more focused narratives.

A struggling frontier colony may appear repeatedly throughout the player's career.

A geologist transported early in the game may later organize an expedition and ask the player to captain it.

A freight operator encountered repeatedly may become a competitor, friend, business partner, or simply a familiar voice over the radio.

Settlements change.

People move.

Businesses succeed and fail.

Children grow up.

New stations appear.

Old ships are retired.

The player's actions contribute to those changes without making the universe revolve around them.

## Time

Time matters.

Journeys take time.

Construction takes time.

Settlements develop over time.

Economic conditions change.

A planet discovered early in the game may look dramatically different years later.

The player's own history becomes visible in the universe.

The tiny freight company that once gave them their first contract may now operate dozens of ships.

The remote settlement they supplied may have become a city.

The player's original starter ship may still be sitting in a hangar somewhere.

The game world remembers progression.

## Risk and Failure

Failure should create stories more often than reload screens.

Running low on fuel may require changing course.

A damaged engine may force an unscheduled landing.

Lost cargo damages reputation and finances.

A poor business decision may require selling equipment.

A failed exploration expedition might force the player to abandon expensive hardware simply to get home.

The most memorable ships may not be perfectly optimized vessels.

They may be machines covered in repairs and compromises because of everything that happened to them.

## Combat

Combat is not the primary activity.

Weapons can exist because piracy, crime, territorial disputes, and dangerous situations exist.

But a commercial freighter should not automatically become a fighter aircraft because someone attacks it.

Avoidance, armor, defensive systems, distress calls, escorts, negotiation, surrendering cargo, and escape should all be legitimate responses.

A heavily armed ship sacrifices cargo capacity, mass, power, and money for capabilities most freight operators rarely need.

Violence is therefore another engineering and economic decision rather than the default solution.

## Visual Identity

Far Haul uses a grounded, industrial, function-over-form science-fiction aesthetic.

Ships look assembled.

Cargo looks heavy.

Machinery looks serviceable.

Components have bolts, access covers, labels, hoses, wiring, connectors and mounting points.

Large vessels resemble industrial infrastructure more than airplanes.

Nothing needs to be aerodynamic unless it regularly enters an atmosphere.

A space-only freighter can simply be an enormous rectangular framework covered in containers, tanks, radiators and engines.

Atmospheric vessels require heat protection, landing gear and appropriate structural design, creating meaningful differences between spacecraft types.

## Sound

Ships should sound like machines.

Pumps cycle.

Fans run.

Relays click.

Bulkheads close.

Metal expands as temperatures change.

Landing gear locks into position with heavy mechanical impacts.

Engines transmit low-frequency vibration through the structure.

The atmosphere changes noticeably when machinery shuts down.

Space itself remains silent.

During EVA, the player primarily hears breathing, suit machinery, radio communication, contact transmitted through the suit, and vibrations conducted through the ship when touching it.

Returning through the airlock restores the familiar mechanical sounds of home.

## Scale

Scale should remain understandable.

Cargo containers are large because the player physically stands beside them.

Engines are enormous because the player can walk around them.

A large freighter feels large because walking from one end to the other takes time.

A planet feels enormous because the player's ship becomes almost insignificant after landing on it.

Space feels enormous because eventually there is nobody nearby to help.

The game doesn't need thousands of interchangeable planets to create scale.

A smaller number of meaningful systems, combined with enormous physical distances and changing levels of human development, can make the universe feel much larger.

## Progression

The fundamental progression of Far Haul is:

Freight → Profit → Ship → Range → Exploration → Discovery → Development → New Freight

The player begins by asking:

How much will someone pay me to take this there?

Later:

Can my ship reach that system?

Eventually:

What's beyond it?

And sometime after that, other people begin asking:

Can you take us there?

The player's career becomes a physical record of humanity moving outward.

At the beginning, the player follows established routes.

Eventually, they help create them.

## Player Fantasy

Far Haul is ultimately about owning a spacecraft and being free to go somewhere.

Not a magical spacecraft.

Not a legendary warship.

A machine.

A machine the player designed, paid for, modified, repaired, slept aboard, landed badly, overloaded, patched together, expanded, and eventually knows well enough to recognize something wrong simply from the sound it makes.

There will always be another shipment.

Another settlement.

Another star.

Another system beyond the edge of the chart.

And if the ship can make it there, the player can find out what's waiting.

Build your ship. Haul the freight. Push the frontier.
