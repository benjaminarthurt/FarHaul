# FAR HAUL

> **Build your ship. Haul the freight. Push the frontier.**

## Overview

**Far Haul** is a first-person open-world science-fiction freight, ship-building, and exploration game set during humanity's expansion across nearby star systems.

The player begins as an independent freight operator with a small, inexpensive ship and enough money to keep it flying. Early life is practical: accept contracts, load cargo, calculate whether a job is worth the fuel, maintain the ship, and move goods between established worlds.

There is no requirement to become a soldier, chosen hero, or savior of civilization.

You are a ship owner.

What happens after that is largely up to you.

Run freight between major industrial worlds. Supply remote colonies. Carry passengers. Salvage abandoned ships. Transport oversized equipment. Build specialized vessels. Survey uninhabited planets. Establish routes through barely explored systems. Or leave the charts entirely and discover places no human has visited before.

The farther you travel, the less infrastructure exists to support you.

Eventually there is no repair yard.

No fuel depot.

No navigation beacon.

No rescue service.

Just your ship and whatever you remembered to bring.

## Core Progression

**Freight → Profit → Ship → Range → Exploration → Discovery → Development → New Freight**

The player begins by asking:

> How much will someone pay me to take this there?

Later:

> Can my ship reach that system?

Eventually:

> What's beyond it?

And sometime after that, other people begin asking:

> Can you take us there?

## The Ship Is the Game

The player's ship is not a menu, inventory screen, or interchangeable vehicle. It is a physical place.

Every compartment built by the player exists inside the game world. The player can leave the pilot's seat and walk through the ship while underway, inspect machinery, enter cargo holds, access engineering spaces, use airlocks, visit crew quarters, and physically reach damaged equipment.

If the ship becomes forty meters longer, it takes longer to walk from the cockpit to engineering. If the player installs another cargo bay, that space physically exists. If a meteor penetrates the hull, the resulting hole exists where the impact occurred. If something outside breaks, someone has to go outside and fix it.

The ship gradually becomes the player's home, workplace, transportation, investment, and record of their journey.

## Function Over Form

Ships in **Far Haul** are unapologetically industrial.

They are boxes, frames, tanks, engines, radiators, cargo racks, antennas, landing gear, pipes, pressure vessels, and machinery.

They are designed to work.

There is no requirement that ships be sleek or symmetrical. A ship only needs to function.

Players can build compact atmospheric freighters, long interstellar cargo vessels, tankers, survey ships, passenger transports, salvage vessels, mobile workshops, or enormous orbital freighters carrying smaller landing craft.

## Freight and Cargo

Freight is the economic foundation of **Far Haul**.

Civilization requires things to move: food, industrial machinery, construction materials, medical supplies, replacement reactor components, raw materials, fuel, scientific equipment, passengers, and oversized cargo.

Cargo has monetary value, mass, physical dimensions, and handling requirements. Some cargo needs refrigeration, shielding, pressure, security, careful handling, or specialized loading equipment.

Interstellar commerce relies heavily on standardized cargo modules. Containers can be moved between ships, stations, surface vehicles, warehouses, and orbital facilities.

## Human Space

Civilization occupies a relatively small region of space.

- **Core systems**: mature economies, heavy traffic, shipyards, customs, large stations, thin margins.
- **Developed systems**: established colonies, mining, manufacturing, regular freight.
- **Frontier systems**: sparse settlements, limited infrastructure, high freight rates.
- **Surveyed systems**: mapped but uninhabited.
- **Unknown systems**: stars known astronomically but never visited by humans.

Eventually, the player reaches the actual edge of humanity's charts.

## Exploration

Beyond established routes, the player's ship becomes the survey mission.

A survey vessel sacrifices cargo capacity for additional fuel, water, food, oxygen reserves, spare parts, redundant systems, scientific equipment, and surface vehicles.

Upon entering an unknown system, sensors construct the map: planets, moons, orbital characteristics, atmospheres, mineral deposits, hazards, and viable landing sites.

Some systems are extraordinary. Others are nearly worthless. Both outcomes matter.

## Planetary Exploration

Many solid planets and moons can be visited directly.

The player can enter orbit, select a landing area, descend, and physically land the ship.

Exploration scales naturally:

**Ship → Surface Vehicle → EVA Suit**

The ship remains where it was left. Range, oxygen, terrain, weather, power, fuel, and the ability to return safely matter.

## Discovery Creates Commerce

Survey data itself has economic value.

The player may discover mineral deposits, water, useful orbital locations, habitable environments, scientific anomalies, or promising settlement sites.

Information can be sold, licensed, published, or retained.

A promising world may initially have:

**Population: 0**

Later scientists arrive.

Then a permanent station.

Then mining.

Then settlers.

Eventually other freight operators establish regular service.

A place the player first visited alone becomes another working part of civilization.

## Physical Ships and Damage

Ships do not simply have a generic health bar.

Damage affects specific systems and locations.

A micrometeorite can penetrate a cargo compartment. Bulkheads close. Pressure drops. The player can isolate the compartment, suit up, inspect the damage, and repair the actual hull.

An engine failure means an engine has failed. A damaged radiator affects cooling. A damaged antenna affects communications. A damaged landing leg affects landing.

## EVA

Spacewalking is a normal part of spacecraft ownership.

Players can use airlocks, handholds, magnetic boots, tethers, and maneuvering equipment to traverse the exterior of their own ship.

EVA can be used to repair hull damage, replace sensors, inspect engines, repair radiators, secure external cargo, investigate objects, board abandoned ships, and perform emergency repairs.

## Economy and Specialization

There is no universally best spacecraft.

Possible specializations include atmospheric freighters, bulk freighters, tankers, survey ships, salvage vessels, passenger/cargo vessels, and heavy transports.

Late-game ships may become too large for routine planetary landing and instead remain in orbit while smaller shuttles transfer freight to the surface.

## Visual Identity

**Far Haul** uses a grounded, industrial, function-over-form science-fiction aesthetic.

Ships look assembled.

Cargo looks heavy.

Machinery looks serviceable.

Components have bolts, access covers, labels, hoses, wiring, connectors, lifting points, and mounting brackets.

Large vessels resemble industrial infrastructure more than airplanes.

Nothing needs to be aerodynamic unless it regularly enters an atmosphere.

## Art Rule

> **Nothing exists merely because it looks futuristic. Every object should look like somebody designed it to perform a job, somebody manufactured it at a price, and eventually somebody will have to repair it.**

## Player Fantasy

**Far Haul** is ultimately about owning a spacecraft and being free to go somewhere.

Not a magical spacecraft. Not a legendary warship.

A machine the player designed, paid for, modified, repaired, slept aboard, landed badly, overloaded, patched together, expanded, and eventually knows well enough to recognize something wrong simply from the sound it makes.

There will always be another shipment.

Another settlement.

Another star.

Another system beyond the edge of the chart.

And if the ship can make it there, the player can find out what's waiting.
