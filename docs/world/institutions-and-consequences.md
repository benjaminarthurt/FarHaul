# Customs, Reputation, Crime, Smuggling, Salvage, and Institutional Relationships

> Canon status: Draft  
> Mechanics sources: `data/world/customs.json`, `crime_law.json`, `smuggling.json`, `salvage_claims.json`, `reputation.json`, and `organization_relationships.json`.

## Information is not universal

These systems share one rule: an institution can react only to information it possesses or can reasonably obtain. A hidden cargo compartment is not automatically known to customs. A private contract failure is not automatically known to every company. An undocumented frontier salvage is not automatically known to a lender. Records propagate through inspections, public legal records, commercial data sharing, direct experience, and imperfect word of mouth.

## Customs

Every inhabited system administers its own customs regime. Customs clearance can involve manifests, duties, permits, quarantine, biosecurity, hazardous declarations, and inspections.

Inspection tools include document checks, mass comparison, container scans, atmospheric trace testing, physical searches, and crew interviews. Systems differ in verification quality and secondary-inspection frequency. A clean history can reduce scrutiny; prior violations, hazardous cargo, biological cargo, registration problems, and mass anomalies increase it.

Customs violations range from correctable manifest mistakes to deliberate concealment.

## Crime

Legal state is jurisdictional. Suspicion, citation, pending case, conviction/adjudication, and dismissal are separate states.

Regulatory failures do not automatically become crimes. An accidental manifest error can remain civil. Deliberately falsifying a manifest, concealing cargo, using a false transponder, converting disputed salvage, or committing contract fraud can create criminal records.

Serious vessel alerts and adjudicated offenses can cross system boundaries through record exchange. Minor violations do not magically become universal knowledge.

## Smuggling

Smuggling is behavior, not a special inventory category. Ordinary legal goods can be smuggled to evade duty. Restricted technology can be concealed from an export rule. Biological material can be moved around quarantine. Stolen cargo can require a complicit buyer.

Methods include false manifests, quantity under-declaration, container tampering, concealed compartments, hidden external mounts, informal-port transfers, and identity substitution.

Each method trades concealment against cost, capacity, mass, registration legitimacy, and the kinds of inspections capable of finding it.

## Salvage and title

Finding a vessel does not automatically make it yours.

A distressed vessel can still belong to its owner. An insurer may acquire recovery rights after paying a total loss. A lender can retain a secured interest. A government can authorize hazard removal. A salvor can earn compensation without receiving title.

Salvage notices record discovery and preserve evidence. Claims can result in a fee, percentage award, title award, return to owner, competing claims, or penalties for unauthorized conversion.

Beyond effective jurisdiction, taking possession can be easy while obtaining clean title is difficult. The problem often appears when the recovered asset is brought back to a registry, reputable yard, insurer, or buyer.

## Reputation

There is no universal player reputation number.

Each organization can hold a relationship record across reliability, care, compliance, credit, and discretion. A captain can be highly trusted by a frontier broker, financially distrusted by a lender, unknown to a government agency, and respected by a salvage company at the same time.

Reputation can propagate through direct experience, commercial data sharing, public legal records, or local word of mouth. Propagation loses strength. Private events remain private unless something causes them to spread.

Good reputation can improve contract access, commercial terms, inspection treatment, credit, or willingness to trust a captain with sensitive work. It never substitutes for a hard legal requirement.

## Institutional relationships

Governments, manufacturers, carriers, cooperatives, standards bodies, survey offices, brokers, and salvage companies have relationships with each other.

Edges describe concrete relationships such as commercial partner, supplier/customer, competitor, data-sharing participant, standards member, or contracting client. They should affect information flow and commercial behavior rather than collapse into two opposing political factions.

A company can compete with another company while buying its equipment. A government can contract with a salvage firm without endorsing everything that firm does. A freight cooperative can share performance data with selected partners without giving every institution access.

This network is intended to make consequences travel through plausible channels rather than through an omniscient reputation system.
