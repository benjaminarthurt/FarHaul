# Known Space — Narrative Overview

Known space in **Far Haul** is not a human empire with alien settlements attached to it. It is an interdependent commercial region formed by several independently developed civilizations whose trade networks have overlapped for generations.

Humans, Kesh, Ilyan, Vey and Orun each retain recognizable home regions, engineering traditions and environmental preferences, but modern commercial space is extensively mixed. A Kesh captain at a human-built freight terminal is ordinary. Human operators install Ilyan engines. Vey life-support equipment is found aboard ships built in New Houston. Old Kesh machinery continues operating decades after its original owners expected to replace it.

The major home systems—**Sol, Keshar, Ilos, Veyara and Orun**—remain enormous economic and cultural centers. **Concord** represents something newer: a deliberately multispecies commercial center where many standards of modern interstellar freight were formalized.

From those centers, development becomes progressively thinner.

New Houston, Hesperus, Iron Gate, Carver, Bradbury and Pelagos are mature specialized economies. Tidemark and Foundry occupy more difficult environments because their resources and industries justify the cost. Port Meridian exists primarily because several routes intersect there. Talos is booming faster than its infrastructure can support. Waystation became a town almost accidentally. Lastlight is the point where ordinary commercial civilization finally runs out.

This geography is fundamental to the game's progression.

A new independent operator can spend years working mature routes without ever becoming an explorer. Cargo flows because different systems actually need one another: Hesperus exports food to industrial populations; Carver supplies metals while importing nearly every ordinary consumer good; New Houston turns resources into ships and machinery; Bradbury moves small quantities of exceptionally valuable scientific freight.

Farther outward, freight becomes less balanced and infrastructure becomes less reliable.

Talos pays extremely well for construction equipment because it is expanding rapidly, but a captain may discover there is almost nothing worth hauling back. Port Meridian can repair nearly anything but charges accordingly. Waystation provides the last comfortable opportunity to fix a questionable system before continuing outward. Lastlight can support an expedition, but it cannot guarantee rescue once that expedition leaves.

Beyond Lastlight, the nature of the game changes without invalidating what came before.

Surveyed but uninhabited systems still depend upon supplies when scientific or mining expeditions arrive. Discoveries create economic information. Economic information attracts investment. Investment creates settlements. Settlements create demand. Demand creates freight routes.

Eventually a place the player once visited alone may appear on an ordinary commercial manifest.

That transformation is one of the central stories of **Far Haul**.

## Civilizational identities

### Humans — Sol

Human commercial engineering is diverse, modular and frequently improvised. Their expansion produced many competing manufacturers and standards. Human operators are particularly comfortable combining equipment that was never originally intended to work together.

### Kesh — Keshar

Kesh engineering reflects a high-gravity homeworld and a culture comfortable with structural margin and redundancy. Their machinery is heavy, conservative and famously durable.

### Ilyan — Ilos

Ilyan engineering grew from a low-gravity environment where mass efficiency became a foundational design value. Their propulsion, sensors and lightweight structural systems are widely respected.

### Vey — Veyara

Vey civilization developed around a largely oceanic homeworld. Their expertise in fluids, environmental management, life support, biotechnology and water systems influences ships throughout known space.

### Orun — Orun

Orun civilization developed around communal subterranean habitation in an environment with severe surface temperature variation. Their machinery is compact, accessible and closely integrated with inhabited spaces.

None of these identities determines an individual's career or personality. They describe histories, environments and engineering traditions—not RPG classes.

## Design intent

Known space should eventually become familiar to the player.

The player should know where to buy an engine, which route usually has food freight, which port charges too much for repairs, where backhaul is difficult, and which controller they expect to hear when approaching a familiar frontier settlement.

Exploration becomes meaningful partly because it contrasts with that familiarity.

When the player finally leaves the network, there is no port they recognize waiting on the other side.
